How to Play Blackjack

Blackjack is the only game on a casino floor where your decisions change the outcome. This page teaches the rules, gives you the complete basic strategy chart, and shows what each house rule actually costs — using numbers measured by simulation, not copied from another site.

The short version: beat the dealer's hand without going over 21. You are not trying to hit 21. You are trying to make a better decision than the dealer is allowed to make — because the dealer is not allowed to make any.

The goal is not to get 21

Almost every beginner gets this wrong, and it costs them money on the very first hand. The object of blackjack is not to reach 21. It is to finish with a total closer to 21 than the dealer, without exceeding 21.

The difference matters because of one asymmetry: if you bust, you lose immediately — even if the dealer busts afterwards. Your money is already in the tray before the dealer turns a card. That single rule is where the entire house edge comes from. Everything else in this page is about managing it.

Card values are simple:

  • Number cards are worth their face value: a 7 is 7.
  • Jack, Queen and King are worth 10. There are sixteen ten-valued cards per deck, which is why so much strategy revolves around them.
  • An Ace is worth 11 or 1, whichever helps you. It changes value automatically as you draw.

A blackjack (also called a natural) is an Ace plus any ten-valued card on your first two cards. It beats any other 21 and normally pays 3 to 2 — bet $10, win $15. Note the word normally. We will come back to it, because it is the single most expensive thing on a casino floor.

How a hand is dealt

  1. You place a bet inside the betting circle, before any cards appear.
  2. The dealer gives every player two cards face up, and takes two: one face up (the up-card) and one face down (the hole card).
  3. If the dealer's up-card is an Ace or a ten, they check the hole card for blackjack. If they have it, the hand ends immediately.
  4. Each player acts in turn, clockwise, choosing from the five options below.
  5. The dealer reveals the hole card and plays out their hand by a fixed rule.
  6. Bets are settled: closer to 21 wins, equal totals are a push (your bet is returned).

Cards are dealt from a shoe holding one to eight decks. A plastic cut card is inserted near the back; when it appears, the current hand finishes and the shoe is reshuffled. How deep that card sits is called penetration, and it is the most important number on the table that nobody advertises.

Hard hands, soft hands, and pairs

You cannot read a strategy chart without these three words. They are the only vocabulary you need.

Soft hand
A hand containing an Ace that can still count as 11 without busting. A,6 is a soft 17. You cannot bust it by taking one card — the worst that happens is the Ace drops to 1. This is why soft hands are played far more aggressively than they look.
Hard hand
Any hand with no Ace, or one where the Ace must count as 1. A 10,7 is a hard 17. So is A,6,10 — the Ace was forced down to 1. Hard 17 and soft 17 are the same number and completely different hands.
Pair
Two cards of the same rank, which you may split into two hands.

The classic mistake: holding A,7, calling it 18, and standing against a dealer 3. It is a soft 18, it cannot bust, and the chart below says to double it.

Your five options

Hit

Take another card. You may keep hitting until you stand or bust.

Stand

Take no more cards and keep your total.

Double down

Double your original bet and receive exactly one more card, then you are done. You use it when you are likely to win but unlikely to improve much beyond one card — typically totals of 9, 10 and 11 against a weak dealer up-card. Some tables only allow doubling on certain totals; this costs you money.

Split

With two cards of the same rank, put up a second bet equal to the first and play two separate hands. Two rules to check at the table: whether you may double after splitting (DAS, good for you), and whether split Aces receive only one card each (almost always yes).

Surrender

Give up the hand and forfeit half your bet. It is offered at a minority of tables and it is the most misunderstood option in the game — most players never use it, and a few use it far too often. It is correct in exactly a handful of spots, all of them marked U in the chart below. If you are holding 16 against a dealer 10, hitting it loses 73.8% of the time and wins 20.3% (measured over four million hands); paying half a bet to walk away is genuinely the cheapest exit.

What the dealer does (and why it is the whole game)

The dealer has no strategy. They follow one written rule, every hand, forever: draw to 16, stand on 17 or more. They cannot double, cannot split, cannot surrender, and cannot decide anything based on your hand.

This is the entire reason basic strategy exists. Because the dealer's behaviour is fixed and public, the probability of every dealer outcome can be computed exactly from their up-card. Your correct play is simply whichever decision has the highest expected value against that distribution — a solvable arithmetic problem with one right answer per situation.

The one variation you must check is what happens on soft 17 (A,6). Two rules exist, usually printed on the felt:

  • S17 — the dealer stands on soft 17. Better for you.
  • H17 — the dealer hits soft 17. Worse for you, and it also changes several of your own correct plays.

H17 gives the dealer a free chance to improve a mediocre hand. Measured on the six-deck game below by changing that single rule and nothing else, H17 costs the player about 0.22 percentage points — the house edge went from 0.35% to 0.57%. It is the second most important rule on the table.

The basic strategy chart

Basic strategy is the complete list of best decisions, assuming you know nothing about the undealt cards. It is not a system, not a betting pattern, and not an opinion — it is the solved answer. Played perfectly it reduces the house edge to a few tenths of one percent, which is the best deal in the building.

How to learn it without memorising 290 cells

You do not memorise the grid. You memorise a handful of rules that generate most of it, then learn the exceptions:

  • Always split Aces and 8s. Never split 10s or 5s. A pair of 10s is already a 20; splitting it turns a near-certain win into two average hands. A pair of 5s is a hard 10 — double it.
  • Dealer shows 2–6: they are weak. They must draw, and they bust often. Stand on your stiff totals (12–16) and let them break.
  • Dealer shows 7–A: they are strong. Assume a 10 in the hole. Hit your stiff totals — yes, even 16, and yes, it feels terrible.
  • Always double hard 11. In this rule set, against every up-card including an Ace.
  • Never take insurance. See below.

The exceptions worth learning explicitly are the surrenders (hard 15 and 16 against big cards, 8,8 against an Ace), soft 18 — which doubles against 2–6 and hits against 9, 10 and A — and 9,9, which splits against everything except 7, 10 and A.

One warning about the total you will hear quoted everywhere: perfect basic strategy does not make blackjack a break-even game. It makes it a game you lose slowly. On the table above, the house keeps about half of one percent of every dollar you put in the circle, forever.

Why insurance is always a bad bet

When the dealer shows an Ace, they offer insurance: a side bet of up to half your original wager, paying 2:1 if the hole card is a ten-valued card. It is pitched as protection. It is a separate bet on a single proposition, and you can price it in one line of arithmetic.

Insurance pays 2:1, so it breaks even when the hole card is a ten one time in three — 33.3%. In a fresh six-deck shoe there are 96 ten-valued cards among 312. With the dealer's Ace and your own two cards visible — neither of them a ten — 309 cards are unknown and 96 of them are tens:

96 ÷ 309 = 31.1%, against a break-even of 33.3%. Expected value: (2 × 0.311) − 0.689 = −0.068.

You lose about 6.8 cents on every dollar of insurance — worse than roulette. It is not protection; it is a second, much worse game offered at a moment of anxiety.

The version that fools the most people is even money. You have a blackjack, the dealer shows an Ace, and they offer you a guaranteed 1:1 payout instead of the 3:2 you might push. That is insurance under another name — and it is the worst possible moment to take it, because one of the tens you need is already in your own hand — a blackjack is an Ace and a ten. The same arithmetic gives 95 ÷ 309 = 30.7%, an expected loss of 7.8% on every dollar of insurance. Decline it. You will lose that hand sometimes. You will still be ahead over a year.

The one exception is a card counter who knows the remaining shoe is unusually rich in tens. That threshold is a specific number, and it appears below.

What each house rule costs

Two tables can look identical and differ by a factor of four in what they take from you. These are the rules to read before you sit down, in order of how much they matter.

1. Blackjack pays 3:2 or 6:5 — check this first, always

A 3:2 blackjack pays $15 on a $10 bet. A 6:5 blackjack pays $12. It sounds like a small trim. It is not.

Measured directly by taking one eight-deck game and changing only the payout — same decks, same H17, same doubling, same splitting — the house edge went from 0.72% to 2.08%. The 6:5 payout alone costs 1.36 percentage points, roughly tripling the price of playing.

No other rule on the felt comes close. A single-deck 6:5 game — advertised as a player's game because single deck sounds generous — is worse than an eight-deck 3:2 game by a wide margin. If the table says 6:5, walk away. This is the single most useful sentence on this page, and it takes two seconds to check.

2. Dealer hits or stands on soft 17

H17 costs about 0.22 percentage points versus S17, measured on the same six-deck game with nothing else changed. Prefer S17.

3. Number of decks

Fewer decks is better for basic strategy, but the effect is small — a few tenths of a point at most — and it is routinely used as bait to sell 6:5 games. Deck count is a tie-breaker, never a reason to accept a worse payout.

4. Doubling, splitting and surrender

Double on any two cards, double after splitting (DAS), re-splitting, and late surrender each help you by roughly 0.1 to 0.2 points. Restrictions such as "double on 10 and 11 only" quietly remove a chunk of your edge.

5. No hole card (common outside North America)

In many European, British and Asian casinos the dealer takes only one card and draws the second after everyone has acted. If they then make blackjack, you lose not only your original bet but everything you doubled and split. Your correct strategy changes: you double and split far less against a 10 or an Ace.

6. Continuous shuffling machines

A CSM returns played cards to the shuffler after every hand. It very slightly improves the house edge for a basic strategy player, so a CSM table is not a bad table. But it deals many more hands per hour, so you lose faster in absolute terms — and it makes card counting structurally impossible, because the composition never drifts.

Card counting, honestly

Card counting is legal. You are doing arithmetic in your head, and no jurisdiction has ever made that a crime. Casinos are private businesses and may ask you to leave, which is a different matter.

The idea is narrow and often overstated. You are not memorising cards or predicting the next one. You are tracking one thing: whether the remaining shoe holds more high cards than low cards. High cards help you — they make dealer stiff hands bust, they make your doubles land, and they make blackjacks, which pay you 3:2 and pay the dealer 1:1.

Hi-Lo, the standard system

Every card gets one of three values. Add them as they appear:

Hi-Lo card values
CardsCountMeaning
2, 3, 4, 5, 6+1Low cards gone — good for you
7, 8, 90Neutral
10, J, Q, K, A−1High cards gone — bad for you

A full deck contains equal numbers of each group, so a complete shoe counts to exactly zero. That is the check: if you count down a deck and do not land on zero, you made an error.

Running count to true count

A running count of +6 means something very different with one deck left than with six. Divide to normalise:

True count = running count ÷ decks remaining

Estimating the undealt decks by eye — from the discard tray — is the skill that separates people who can count from people who know the values. It is harder than the arithmetic and it is the part almost nobody practises.

The deviations, and insurance at +3

When the count is high, a few basic strategy decisions flip. The best known set is the Illustrious 18. Rather than reprint them, this application recomputes them from the rules — by distorting the shoe composition until it carries a given true count, then finding the crossover point by bisection.

The published set is recovered 17 out of 17, each to within one unit. A few of them:

  • Insurance becomes profitable at true count +3 (exact crossover 3.34). This is the single most valuable deviation, and the only time the answer to the insurance section above is yes.
  • Hard 16 against a 10: stand at true count 0 or higher, hit below.
  • Hard 15 against a 10: stand at +4.
  • Hard 12 against a 3: stand at +1.
  • Hard 10 against a 10: double at +3.

Deviations are a small part of the edge. The large part is betting more when the count is high and the minimum when it is not — which is also the part that gets you noticed.

Penetration: the number nobody advertises

The cut card decides how much of the shoe is dealt before a reshuffle. Deal 75% of six decks and you play about four and a half decks; deal 85% and you play just over five. Casinos do not post this figure, and it is the single most important table condition for a counter.

Here is the distinction almost every page gets wrong: penetration barely changes the house edge for a basic strategy player. If you are not counting, a deep cut card is worth almost nothing to you. What it changes is how often the count reaches a level worth betting into. A shallow shoe is reshuffled just as the composition starts to drift — which is precisely why casinos that suspect counters move the cut card forward rather than change any posted rule.

The same logic explains a result that surprises people: a two-deck game with mediocre rules is often more profitable to count than an eight-deck game with excellent ones. Fewer decks means the true count swings further and faster. For a counter, the geometry of the shoe outweighs a tenth of a point of house edge.

The honest ceiling

This is where most blackjack pages stop, and it is the part that matters.

A card counter's edge is small. Perfect play — the reference ramp built into this application, betting two units for every point of true count above +1, plus insurance and the count deviations — was measured at about +0.008 units per hand on the six-deck Strip game. This site does not turn that into an hourly figure, and you should distrust any site that does: it would take a hands-per-hour rate nobody here measures.

The swings are the real story. Blackjack's per-hand standard deviation, measured on the same table, is 2.6 units per hand — more than three hundred times the edge it is supposed to reveal. Losing for weeks while playing flawlessly is not bad luck; it is the ordinary behaviour of the game. This is why serious players talk about bankroll before they talk about strategy, and why "having an edge" and "making money" are separated by months of variance and a great deal of capital.

And it only works at all under conditions that are increasingly rare: 3:2 payouts, no continuous shuffler, and a cut card placed deep enough to matter. Two of the ten rule sets above cannot be beaten by any counting system, at any skill level, ever.

Treat card counting as a difficult skill worth learning for its own sake — not as an income. Anyone selling it as an income is selling you something.

Common questions

Is counting cards illegal?

No. Keeping a mental tally of cards that have been dealt in front of you is not a crime anywhere. Casinos are private property and can refuse service, so counters get asked to leave or barred — but that is a business decision, not a legal one. Using a device or an accomplice to help you count is illegal in many jurisdictions, including Nevada.

Does another player's bad play cost me money?

No, and this is the most persistent myth at the table. When the player next to you hits a 15 and takes "the dealer's bust card", they were equally likely to have taken a card that would have saved you. Over any meaningful number of hands the effect is exactly zero. Their decisions change the order of the cards, not the odds. Play your own hand.

Why do I have to hit 16 against a 10? I always bust.

You do, 61.5% of the time. But if you stand you win only when the dealer busts, which they do about 23% of the time when showing a 10 (having already checked for blackjack). Hitting is not a good play — it is the less bad of two losing plays. If the table offers surrender, the chart says to take it, which is the cheapest of the three.

Should I always split 8s, even against a 10?

Yes — with one exception in this rule set. A hard 16 is the worst hand in blackjack; two hands starting with an 8 are both better than one hand of 16, even against a strong up-card. You are not splitting to win, you are splitting to lose less. The exception: against an Ace at an H17 table with surrender available, the chart says surrender instead.

Can you count cards against a continuous shuffling machine?

No. A CSM returns cards to the shuffler after every hand, so the composition never drifts away from a full shoe and the true count stays pinned near zero. There is nothing to track. No counting system defeats a CSM.

Does basic strategy mean I will win?

No. Basic strategy is the best available play, and the best available play still loses money slowly at every table listed above. It reduces the house edge from roughly 2% for an average player to a few tenths of a point. That is the whole benefit, and it is worth having — but it is a reduction in cost, not a profit.

What is the best blackjack table to look for?

In order: 3:2 blackjack (non-negotiable), dealer stands on soft 17, double on any two cards, double after splitting, surrender available, and fewer decks. Check the payout first. It outweighs everything else combined.

Is there a betting system that beats blackjack?

No. Martingale, Paroli, D'Alembert and every other progression change the shape of your results — many small wins and rare catastrophic losses, or the reverse — without changing the expected value by a single cent. No sequence of bet sizes can turn a negative-expectation game positive. Only changing the odds does that, which is what counting attempts and why it is hard.

Play responsibly

Everything on this page is about losing less, not winning. Blackjack has a built-in mathematical advantage for the house on every table described here. Played perfectly, over a long enough period, you lose. That is the design of the game and no strategy on this page changes it.

Set a limit before you sit down, treat it as the price of an evening, and stop when it is gone. Never gamble money you need, and never gamble to recover losses — chasing is the single most reliable way to turn a bad night into a serious problem.
